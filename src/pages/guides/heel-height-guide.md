Heel height gets discussed as a style question. It is mostly a physics question. The number in the product description determines how steeply your foot is angled, how much of your weight lands on your forefoot, and how much continuous work your ankle does to keep you upright.

Get the height right for the occasion and the shoe disappears. Get it wrong and no amount of quality rescues it.

## The One Concept Worth Understanding: Pitch

Pitch is the difference between the heel height and any platform under the front of the shoe. It is what your foot actually experiences, and it is why two shoes with the same stated heel height can feel completely different.

- A 70 mm heel with a flat sole has 70 mm of pitch.
- A 90 mm heel on a 20 mm platform has 70 mm of pitch — the same angle, at a visually taller height.

If you want height without the cost, a hidden platform is the most effective single feature to look for. Brands rarely advertise it clearly, so check the side profile in product photos.

### 30–50 mm — the kitten heel

Enough rise to change the line of your leg and the posture of an outfit, little enough that your forefoot never really notices. The most underrated height, and the one to buy if you want a heel you'll genuinely wear on a Tuesday.

A kitten heel is slim by definition. If you want this height with more stability, look for a low block instead — same rise, far wider base.

### 50–70 mm — the practical sweet spot

This is where most of the good Scandinavian design lives, and where a well-built block heel becomes something you can wear through a full working day. High enough to read as a heel, low enough that pitch stays manageable.

If you are buying one pair of heels and want it to be useful, buy in this range on a block. Our [block heel edit](/edit/best-scandinavian-block-heels) is almost entirely 50–70 mm for this reason.

### 70–90 mm — the evening height

Genuinely tall, and where the compromises start. On a block base with a small platform, a fit person can do four or five hours. On a stiletto with no platform, plan for less and plan for sitting.

### 90 mm and above — occasion only

At this pitch, the majority of your body weight moves onto the forefoot and your ankle works continuously. There is no construction that makes this comfortable for a full day — the good versions simply make it survivable for an evening. Buy it for how it looks, and be honest with yourself about the schedule.

## Height Is Only Half of It — Base Shape Is the Other Half

At identical height, the base changes the shoe completely:

- **Stiletto** — a single point. Maximum ankle-stabilisation work, minimum contact area. The most fatiguing base at any height.
- **Block** — a wide, squared column. Spreads load and removes most of the stabilisation demand. The best all-round choice.
- **Column / cylinder** — nearly as stable as a block, slightly more elegant in profile.
- **Wedge** — the most stable of all, because the sole is continuous. Least formal.

The practical rule: **going from a stiletto to a block buys you more comfort than dropping 20 mm of height.** If a shoe has to work for a long day, change the base before you compromise on the height you actually want.

## Matching Height to Occasion

- **Standing work, travel, daily wear** — 20–50 mm, block or low stack.
- **Office and long professional days** — 50–70 mm block. Vagabond and Flattered both do this range well.
- **Weddings and long events** — 50–70 mm block with a platform if you can find one. You will be standing far longer than you expect.
- **Dinner, evenings out** — 70–90 mm, any base you like.
- **Seated occasions** — whatever you want.

## What to Buy at Each Height

- **Kitten and low** — Flattered and Vagabond both do restrained low heels well.
- **50–70 mm block** — the heart of the Nordic range: Flattered, ATP Atelier, Vagabond, Roccamore.
- **70–90 mm** — Aeyde and Anonymous Copenhagen, which lean taller and more architectural.
- **All-day at any height** — [Roccamore](/edit/roccamore-vs-flattered), whose insole does more work than the height suggests.
